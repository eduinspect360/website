A note for inspectorates

What a modern inspection platform has to get right

Most systems sold to ministries manage schools. Very few manage the inspection of schools — a different problem, with an evidence trail, an approval chain and a publication obligation at the end of it — and these are the five tests that decide whether a platform is genuinely useful.

Read the five tests For national inspectorates
Test 1 of 5 · The approval chain

The approval chain is the hard part — not the visit.

The school visit is the visible work. The difficulty lives afterwards: getting a draft report from a lead inspector, through quality assurance, back to the school for response, through senior sign-off, and out to publication — with a defensible record of who decided what, and when.

A platform earns its place here or nowhere, and the test is whether it handles the unhappy path:

Rejection and resubmission

A report rejected at QA and sent back for revision, then resubmitted — without losing the original judgements or the audit trail.

Response tied to the judgement

A school's formal response to findings, captured against the specific judgement it contests — not bolted on as a loose comment.

Status that is never in doubt

Any inspector can see exactly where any report sits, and what is blocking it — an unambiguous status at every stage.

A useful diagnostic: when evaluating any system, ask to see a report rejected and resubmitted. If the demonstration only shows a report moving cleanly forward, it has shown you the easy 20%.
Test 2 of 5 · Evidence traceability

Every judgement must trace back to its evidence.

A judgement that cannot be traced to the observation behind it is indefensible the moment it is challenged — by a school, a ministry, or an auditor. This is the single most important property of an inspection record, and the easiest to lose when a paper-and-spreadsheet process is digitised carelessly.

Judgement linked to evidence

Each judgement links to the specific evidence — lesson observations, document review, interviews — that produced it.

The link survives time

It survives editing, re-grading and the passage of years, so a report defended long after publication still holds together.

Captured on site

Evidence is captured at the point of observation — not reconstructed from memory days later.

Test 3 of 5 · AI governance

AI can draft. It must never decide.

AI is genuinely useful in inspection — compiling school context, structuring collected evidence, turning an inspector's notes into well-formed draft prose. It removes administrative weight, and that weight is considerable. But its role has a hard boundary, and a credible platform enforces it in the architecture, not in a policy document.

Named human approval

Every AI-generated output requires named human approval before it affects any published outcome. Nothing reaches a school automatically.

The inspector decides

The judgement is always the inspector's. AI structures and drafts; it does not grade.

Append-only audit log

Every AI action is written to an append-only audit log — what was generated, by what, and who signed it off.

This matters more each year. A government inspectorate must be able to answer, to a minister or an auditor: where was AI used, and who was accountable for the result? A platform that cannot answer that cleanly is a liability, however capable it appears.
Test 4 of 5 · Data sovereignty

Data must be sovereign — and provably segregated.

Inspection data is among the most sensitive an education system holds: named pupils, identifiable staff, judgements with real consequences. Three things are non-negotiable.

Architectural segregation

Segregation is architectural, not procedural. Where a platform serves more than one client, one jurisdiction's data must be structurally incapable of informing another's — not merely a rule an administrator is trusted to remember.

Residency is your decision

Where the data is hosted, and under whose jurisdiction, is the inspectorate's call — not the supplier's.

Exit defined before entry

Full export in open formats and a documented path out, agreed at the start. Data a supplier can hold hostage was never sovereign.

Test 5 of 5 · Framework configurability

The framework is yours — the platform must bend to it.

Every inspectorate is different: its evaluation areas, its grading scale, its inspection categories, its approval routes, its report structure. A platform hardcoded to one country's model forces every other client to adopt that model — which no serious inspectorate will, nor should.

The wrong question

“Does it match our framework today?” — a snapshot that flatters a demo but says nothing about what happens when the framework moves on, as every inspectorate's framework eventually does.

The right question

“When our framework changes — and it will — is that a configuration change, or a redevelopment project?” A framework revision should not stall published output for a year while the system catches up.

In one line

What good looks like.

A defensible judgement, traceable to its evidence, moved through an approval chain that survives challenge, assisted by AI that never decides, on data the inspectorate wholly controls, within a framework that is genuinely its own.

None of this is exotic. But it is the part that is hard to build and easy to get wrong — and it is worth testing for directly before committing to any system, this one included.
